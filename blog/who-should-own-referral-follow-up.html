Who should own referral follow-up in a small practice?

Foram DoshiOctober 26, 2026 · 4 min read

A referral arrives by fax at twenty to five on a Tuesday. The front desk prints it and puts it in the pile for whoever opens the schedule in the morning. The medical assistant picks it up Wednesday, notices the insurance needs checking, and leaves it for the biller. The biller clears it Thursday afternoon. On Friday the referring office’s nurse calls to ask whether anyone there received it, because the patient rang her, annoyed.

Everyone in that chain did their job. Nobody’s job was the referral.

That is not a diligence problem. It is an ownership problem, and it is usually fixable in an afternoon of deciding — no software required.

01Follow-up is four jobs, not one

“Referral follow-up” sounds like one task. Pull it apart and there are four, with different rhythms and different skills.

Intake and logging — the referral exists in a list, spelled the same way every time, with the referring provider recorded rather than typed from memory. Patient contact — reaching the person, getting them scheduled, closing the loop when they do not answer. The report back out — getting your findings to the referring provider, which is the part referring offices judge you on. The relationship view — noticing that an office which used to send two a month has sent nothing since August.

The first three are tasks. The fourth is attention, which is harder to assign and is nearly always the one nobody holds.

02Why “the front desk” is usually the wrong answer

The front desk is interrupt-driven work. There is a phone ringing and a patient standing at the window, and both of those are, correctly, more urgent than a referral that came in yesterday.

Referral follow-up is queue work. Nothing visibly bad happens today if it slips, and interrupt work always wins against queue work when one person holds both — there is a human being waiting in front of them. Handing the queue to the interrupt desk is not delegation. It is a slow decision not to do the work, in a form that lets everyone feel it has been handled.

One exception: if your front desk has genuinely protected time on the calendar, this can work. Most do not.

03Why “everyone” is worse

Work that belongs to everyone belongs to whoever feels most guilty about it that week. That is not a system. It is a personality trait doing a job.

A shared inbox is the usual shape of this. Three people can see the referral, and each assumes one of the others has it — because each of them has, at some point, been the one who picked it up. Read and not actioned is indistinguishable from unread until the nurse calls on Friday.

The other cost is quieter. When nobody owns the work, nobody can be asked about it. You cannot usefully ask a group why four offices went quiet. You can ask a person, in ten minutes — and she usually knows.

04Five questions to settle this week

Five questions, settled this week
  1. Name one person for the report back out. Not a team, not a role in the abstract. If you settle only one of the four jobs, settle this one — it is what a referring provider notices, and what decides whether they send you the next patient.
  2. Write down what “done” means. Patient contacted within one business day; scheduled, or logged as unreachable after an agreed number of attempts; findings back to the referring provider within a set number of days of the visit. Use your own numbers. The point is that two people would agree on whether a given referral is finished.
  3. Give that person a protected block. Weekly, on the calendar, defended the way a clinic session is defended. Ownership without time is just a name to blame later.
  4. Make the list visible to one other person. The owner looks weekly; the administrator or physician glances at it monthly. Work owned privately and invisibly can stop without anyone deciding to stop it.
  5. Work out what the job is worth before deciding you cannot afford it. Count last month’s referrals, multiply by what a new referred patient is worth to your practice, then ask what share of that you lose to two weeks of silence. Your figures, not an industry average.

05When nobody has the hours

“Nobody has the hours” is often simply true in a practice of seven people. Three honest options, cheapest first.

Shrink the job to its highest-value slice and say so — reports back out within the week, nothing else formally owned for now. Or trade it against something you stop doing, and name the thing you are stopping, because a trade nobody names is not a trade. Or move the parts that need no judgment off a person: a list that cannot hold one office under four spellings, a prompt when a referral has sat untouched for five days, a monthly count that assembles itself. The build on our work page took six weeks and did exactly that — it decided nothing for the clinic, it just stopped the remembering from being a person’s job.

Automating judgment does not work. Automating remembering works well.

One possibility worth checking before any of it: someone may already own this in practice. She maintains the spreadsheet, she notices when an office goes quiet, and she has never been told it is her job or given an hour to do it in. Naming what is already happening costs nothing and is sometimes the whole fix.
