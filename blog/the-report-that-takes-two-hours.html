The report that takes two hours every month

Foram DoshiNovember 2, 2026 · 4 min read

It is the last Thursday of the month. Someone in your office has the EHR open in one window and a spreadsheet in the other. She exports the month’s referrals, pastes them in, deletes the columns nobody reads, fixes the four spellings of the same referring office, counts how many are still open, highlights the ones that have gone past two weeks, and types the totals into the same three-row summary she has typed every month since March. Around eleven she sends it to you.

It is accurate. It took her the better part of two hours. You read it in ninety seconds and the meeting moves on.

Nothing there is anyone’s fault. But it is worth naming what happened: a capable person spent two hours on a job with no judgment in it at all.

01The judgment is five minutes. The assembling is the rest.

Pull apart any monthly report and you find two different kinds of work.

Assembling is exporting, cleaning, matching names, counting, formatting. It has one right answer. Two careful people would produce the same number, and so would a computer.

Interpreting is noticing that two referring offices dropped off in the same fortnight, that the backlog sits in one provider’s schedule and not the other’s, and deciding whether anyone should pick up the phone about it. That is the part worth paying a person for, and it is almost always the shorter half.

Most of the hours go to the half with no judgment in it. And when the month gets busy, it is the interpreting that gets dropped — the report still goes out, because the report is the visible part. The thinking quietly does not happen.

02Three tests for a report that should not be manual

Not every manual report is worth changing. Run these three, in order.

Does anyone act on it? If nothing has changed in six months because of this report, the fix is not to automate it. The fix is to stop producing it, and that one is free.

Would two people building it get the same number? If yes, it is assembling, and assembling can be moved off a person. If two capable people would disagree — because one counts a referral as closed when the patient is scheduled and the other when the report goes back out — then you do not have a reporting problem yet. You have a definition problem, and no software fixes that.

Is the input a field, or is it prose? A date, a dropdown, a provider identifier: countable. “Called, left msg w/ daughter, will try Tues” is readable by a person and invisible to a computer. Reports built on prose cannot be automated. They can only be retyped. That is usually the real reason the two hours exist.

03Work out what it costs on your own numbers

A report that takes two hours to assemble is not a reporting problem. It is a record-keeping problem arriving a month late.

Before you ask what a fix would cost, work out what the current arrangement costs. Two figures, both from your own practice.

The first: the hourly cost of the person who builds it — wage plus the share you pay on top — times the hours, times twelve.

The second is usually larger and never appears on a budget. What did she not do in those hours? If the person assembling the report is also the person who calls the patients nobody has reached, then the cost of the report is measured in unreached patients, not in wages.

Write both down. If the total is small, keep the spreadsheet — genuinely. A two-hour report in a practice of five people is not an emergency, and people who sell software have an incentive not to say so.

04Four checks before you automate anything

Four checks, before you automate
  1. Write the definition down first. One sentence per number: what counts as an open referral, when the clock starts, when it stops. If you cannot write it in a sentence, no system can count it.
  2. Trace each number back to a field. Go line by line and find where each figure actually comes from. If it comes from someone reading free-text notes, the first project is structure, not reporting.
  3. Decide who reads it and when. A report with no standing slot on a calendar becomes a report nobody reads automatically instead of manually. Name the meeting before you name the metric.
  4. Run the manual version alongside for a month. Build it, keep producing the old one once more, and compare. Where the two disagree, your definition was fuzzier than you thought — and finding that out in week three is cheaper than finding it out in a physician meeting.

05The first build is usually structure, not the report

When a practice asks us for a dashboard, the dashboard is usually the second project. The first is making the record countable: one entry per referring office rather than four spellings, call attempts stored as dated entries rather than a sentence, a field for the thing you keep counting by hand.

That is most of what the referral portal on our work page did in its first six weeks. It did not add reporting. It made the clinic’s own calling rule countable, because the attempts were finally entries instead of prose. Once that is true, the monthly count stops being a task and becomes a question you can ask whenever you want the answer.

Sometimes the conclusion is that nothing should be built. If the report drives no decisions, stop writing it. If the definitions are contested, settle them in a meeting rather than in a build. Both are free.
